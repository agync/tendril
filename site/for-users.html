For people using an app

Your app uses Tendril

An app on your devices says it is secured by Tendril. Here is what that means for your data, in plain words, including what it cannot promise.

01

What it does for you

Your devices talk to each other, not to an account in someone's cloud. What passes between them is sealed, so only those two devices can read it.

  • No account

    Tendril pairs your devices with a code, not a sign-in.

  • Nobody in between reads it

    Not your network, not a relay, not the app's maker.

  • Keys stay on your devices

    They are made there. The maker's servers never hold them.

The details

The seal. Each connection starts with a handshake, Noise_XXhfs_25519+MLKEM768_ChaChaPoly_SHA256, that proves each device holds the keys it paired with, then encrypts everything after it. A path counts only once that proof succeeds.

Paths. On the same network the devices connect directly. Away from home they can try hole punching through iroh, and if nothing direct gets through, a relay forwards sealed frames. A relay can be the maker's, a public one, or one you run.

Keys. Each device makes its own keys. On desktops they sit in files only your user account can read. On phones they are sealed with a key from the Keychain or the Android Keystore, stay on that phone, and are not restored to another device from a backup. The Swift and Android test suites do not yet run in CI.

02

What you will see

Pairing happens once per device and takes three steps.

Why compare? If the strings differ, someone is between your devices. Cancel, and that device gets no access.

After that, there is nothing more to do. Your phone finds the laptop on its own, and you are not asked which way to connect.

  • At home, directly over your Wi-Fi.
  • Away, through your VPN if you use one.
  • Anywhere else, through a relay that cannot read what it passes on.
  • If the laptop's address changes, it tells your phone.
The details

The code. It is bound to this one handshake and never crosses the network itself. The QR code also carries the laptop's key, so the phone turns away an impostor before it says anything. Pairing across the internet uses a longer code, TND-XXXX-XXXX-XXXX.

The addresses. A QR code also carries the laptop's addresses, signed by the laptop, and your phone keeps them. Anyone who photographs the code sees those addresses until it expires. If your phone ever cannot reach the laptop at all, the app may ask you to scan a fresh code. That updates the addresses and keeps the pairing.

The string. Both devices work it out from the handshake they just ran. A device in the middle would have run a different handshake with each side, so the two screens would not match.

After pairing. The new device can open a sealed connection and nothing else, until the app grants it something.

03

What you control

The laptop keeps the list of your devices and decides what each may do. A new device can do nothing until the app allows it.

  • Permissions can run out on their own, for example after an evening.
  • Lost a phone? Remove it on the laptop. Its next request is refused, even mid-connection, and it cannot connect again.
  • Remove it soon. Until you do, an unlocked phone can still do what it was allowed.
The details

The list. The device that runs the app keeps a roster: every device it has paired, whether each is pending, active or removed, and its grants. A grant is a signed permission with an end time.

Checks. The app asks Tendril before it acts on a request. The answer is no unless an active device holds a grant that covers it.

Removing. A removed device is marked revoked and its grants are cleared, so its next request is denied, even in a connection that is already open. It cannot open a new one, or pair again with the same key. A phone that is offline learns of it when it next connects.

Read more: Grants and Revocation on How it works

04

What it cannot promise

Tendril protects what you send. These four things it does not, or not yet.

Who talks to whom
Networks and relays still see your devices' addresses, when they connect and how much they send. They cannot see what.
New code
Tendril is new. Its code has had no outside security review yet.
Future quantum computers
The link uses two kinds of encryption together, a long-standing one and a newer one designed to resist quantum computers. It stays secure as long as either one holds.
The app itself
The app on your devices still sees your data, as it must to show it to you. Tendril protects the link, not the app. Ask the app's maker what it does with your data.
The details

Metadata. By default, a relay sees the same mailbox key each time a device waits for connections, so it can link that device's sessions, and exact message sizes and regular keepalives show patterns. An app can turn on Wire v2, which changes that key every hour, rounds message sizes up to fixed steps and varies the timing of keepalives. Even then, networks see when your devices talk and roughly how much.

Hybrid post-quantum. The handshake pairs X25519 with ML-KEM-768, and signatures pair Ed25519 with ML-DSA-65. Two parts are not covered: relay mailbox proofs use Ed25519 only, and the iroh transport under the peer-to-peer path is classic. Your data inside the session is covered on every path.

Waking a phone. An app can wake a sleeping phone through a push service such as Apple's or Google's. The push carries only a tag that changes every hour, never your data, device names or keys. The push service still sees that your phone was woken, and when.

Review. The algorithms are standard. The Noise code that runs the handshake is Tendril's own, checked against a separate Go version, and not yet audited.
